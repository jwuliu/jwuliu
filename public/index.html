About me

Welcome! My name is Jasen Liu and I am a Biodiversity Genomics Postdoctoral Fellow at the Smithsonian Environmental Research Center, working with Melissa McCormick. I got my PhD from the Population Biology Graduate Group at UC Davis, where I worked with Santiago Ramírez.

I am an organismal biologist fascinated by how biotic interactions shape evolutionary processes. Most of my work focuses on a particularly amazing group of plants - the orchids - which often exhibit highly specialized relationships with their pollinators and fungal symbionts. My research addresses how these interactions affect evolution at different scales, from the genetics underlying interaction-mediating traits, to how they influence speciation and macroevolutionary dynamics. Above all, I am motivated by natural history, which provides the foundation for my work.

Research

For a full list of publications, please see Google Scholar.

My work explores how biotic interactions affect evolutionary processes, with a primary focus on how they have shaped the extraordinary orchid radiation. Orchids are one of the most diverse families of plants, with over 20,000 species distributed throughout the world that exhibit an amazing array of cross-kingdom interactions. I investigate how aspects of these interactions have contributed to the accumulation of species and trait diversity across this family, integrating phylogenetic comparative methods, lab experiments, analytical chemistry, and molecular genetics to make sense of processes across levels of biological organization.

Current research questions I am pursuing include:

  1. How do fungal interactions structure species boundaries?

    All orchids begin their lives as mycoheterotrophs, relying completely on interactions with mycorrhizae before they gain photosynthetic abilities (or in some extreme cases, their whole lives). Despite major differences in fungal associates across orchids, including between closely-related species, the role that fungi play in mediating species boundaries are unclear. I am using in vitro germination experiments between closely-related species of North American Platanthera orchids and in their hybrids to characterize the role in which fungal interactions contribute to reproductive isolation.

  2. How do pollinators shape the macroevolutionary landscape of floral traits?

    The complex pollination mechanisms of orchids have captured the attention of biologists since the dawning of evolutionary science. Key to these interactions are signals such as scent and color that target animal sensory biases and morphological traits that facilitate contact with the reproductive organs. I am interested in the ways in which pollinators exert selection on flowers to promote or constrain the accumulation of phenotypic diversity. I have previously investigated how independent origins of male euglossine bee-pollination have influenced evolution of floral morphology and scent chemistry across tropical American orchids.

  3. How does contingency alter evolutionary trajectories of traits?

    The potential evolutionary trajectories available to a lineage are constrained by its evolutionary history. I am fascinated by how contingency operates to affect the size of potential steps lineages can make in the phenotypic landscape, particularly with respect to traits mediating biotic interactions. For example, certain groups of orchids are capable of extremely rapid floral scent evolution while others exhibit apparent stasis in chemistry. I am interested in investigating the role of contingency in generating trait diversity by characterizing gene expression and gene family evolution across clades varying with respect to traits. My ultimate goal is to understand the ways in which the extensive phenotypic novelty in contemporary orchids has been generated.
